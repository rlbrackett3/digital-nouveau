**Prescient Dystopia:** Written ~30 years prior, the novel's depiction of societal collapse, climate crisis, and fragile suburban enclaves feels uncomfortably close to current reality. The author notes it's "almost too close to reality to remain fiction."

**Philosophy of Change:** Butler replaces traditional religion with a belief in **Change** as the universe's life force and resistance to entropy. The core tenet is to embrace uncertainty, prepare for the unexpected, and actively shape change rather than despair over it.

**Cycle of Destruction & Rebirth:** The narrative frames destruction → trauma → healing → rebirth as an eternal cycle. Despite profound sorrow, there is underlying optimism: accepting uncertainty leads to freedom.

> "Change as the life force of the universe, and the resistance to entropy that keeps the gears turning. Embrace this change, prepare for the unexpected, and lean into uncertainty."

> "Destruction leads to trauma, trauma to healing, and eventual to rebirth. The cycle of suffering and happiness is as eternal as change."

**Real-World Paralleles:** 
- The "Pyros" (drug-fueled anarchists burning the landscape) mirror modern California wildfires and ecological collapse.
- Subtle societal shifts causing ripple effects parallel William Gibson's *Periphery* and *Agency*.
- The author notes the ironic/temporal uncanniness of the book's themes aligning with modern political movements.

**Group Discussion:**
- Central question: *What would Butler think of this story now?* Are we currently in the "Pox" (the slide into collapse) or on the threshold of a reset?
- Group sentiments included: "It's weird" and "I don't know truly whether I liked it or not."
